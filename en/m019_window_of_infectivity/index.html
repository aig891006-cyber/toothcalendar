Is the "Window of Infectivity" Real? Shared Spoons and Cavity Germs

The idea that cavity germs spread only at 19 to 31 months is outdated. How germs really spread, spoons and kisses, and what matters: sugar, fluoride, brushing.

At a glance

  • Cavity germs spread all the time, not just in one window
  • Cutting sugar, fluoride, and brushing matter more than germs
  • Getting your own cavities treated helps your child too

"Can sharing a spoon spread cavities?" "Is it okay to kiss my baby?" If you read up on parenting, you've probably come across the term "window of infectivity." The idea was that guarding one specific period could help a child avoid cavities, but thinking has changed a lot since then. Here's the old idea, today's view, and what really matters at home.

What the "window" idea was, and how it's seen now

A study in the 1990s found that cavity germs (mutans streptococci) first settled in children's mouths mostly between 19 and 31 months of age. This period was called the "window of infectivity," and the idea spread widely that guarding these few months was enough.

As more research came in, the picture changed.

  • Germs are there even before teeth come in. Cavity germs have been found in the mouths of babies with no teeth yet, especially on the tongue.
  • They don't only come from mom. They can also spread from close contacts like dads, siblings, grandparents, and daycare friends.
  • Spread is ongoing, not limited to a few months. So today, rather than a set "window," germs are seen as spreading little by little throughout early childhood.

The bigger change is in how cavities are understood. Cavities used to be seen like an infection caught from mom. Now, germ spread is seen as just one cause among many. Cavities don't come from germs alone. How often your child eats sugary food, whether fluoride is used, and how well the teeth are brushed all play a part. It's also known that many kinds of bacteria are involved, not just mutans.

That's why recent guidelines focus on cutting sugar, fluoride, and brushing, plus regular dental checkups, more than on blocking germ spread.

Here's what to focus on at home

There's no way to completely stop germs from spreading. Reducing the conditions that make cavities likely matters much more. Here's what to do, most important first.

  1. Get your own cavities and gum disease treated. A caregiver's oral health affects the child, for better or worse. Getting dental care yourself is prevention for your child, too.
  2. Avoid added sugar before age two. Foods with added sweetness, like cookies and sweet drinks, can wait.
  3. Gradually cut back on night feeds and frequent grazing. The more often food goes into the mouth, the less of a break the teeth get. Cutting back a little every few days, rather than all at once, helps your child adjust.
  4. Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste. Use a rice-grain smear under age 3 and a pea-sized amount from age 3. Never skip brushing before bed.
  5. If you can, use separate spoons and toothbrushes. It's also best not to pre-chew food for your child.
  6. Keep up regular dental checkups. Cavity risk differs from child to child, so checkups help you find the right prevention plan for yours.

Separate spoons are near the bottom of the list on purpose. They're worth trying, but no study has directly shown that avoiding shared spoons or kisses reduces cavities. A grandparent tasting food before feeding your child, or a kiss, won't give your child cavities, so don't worry too much.

The more common mistake is actually the opposite. Some parents carefully keep spoons separate, but let their child carry a straw cup of juice around all day or skip brushing before bed. Those habits have a much bigger effect on cavities.

Frequently asked questions

Can kissing my child spread cavity germs?

Germs can pass through saliva. But no study has directly shown that avoiding kisses reduces cavities. Show all the affection you want, and focus on how often your child has sweets and on brushing.

My child is past 31 months. Can I relax now?

Cavity germs are seen as spreading all the time, not just at a certain age. And cavities depend far more on eating habits and brushing than on germs, so keep up the same care no matter your child's age.

If a parent has lots of cavities, will the child get them too?

Not necessarily, but a caregiver's oral health does affect the child. Families also tend to share foods and daily habits. If parents get treated first and the whole family cuts back on sweet snacks, it helps the child too.

Do I have to completely stop sharing spoons?

No need. Using separate ones when you can, and not pre-chewing food, is enough. Slipping up on some days won't lead straight to cavities.

When to see a dentist

  • You see a white band or a chalky, dull patch near the gums on the front teeth
  • A brown or black spot appears on a tooth
  • Siblings or family members have lots of cavities and you're worried about your child's risk
  • It's been 6 months since the first tooth and you haven't been to the dentist yet
  • You, the caregiver, have untreated cavities or gum disease (see a dentist yourself too)
