Why Treat Baby Teeth That Will Fall Out Anyway? What Untreated Cavities Can Do

Baby molars stay until age 10 to 12, chewing and holding space for adult teeth. What untreated cavities can do, how they're managed now, and when not to wait.

At a glance

  • Baby molars stay until age 10-12, chewing and holding space
  • Untreated cavities can hurt and even affect permanent teeth
  • Not every cavity needs drilling right away; get it checked

"Why treat a tooth that's going to fall out anyway?" Parents ask this a lot. When your child is still little and you think treatment might be hard on them, it's even harder to decide. Here's what baby teeth do, what can happen if a cavity is left alone, and how cavities are managed today.

What baby teeth do, and what happens if you wait

Baby teeth may feel like they're gone in no time, but they work longer than you'd think. The front teeth come out fairly early, but the baby molars stay until around age 10 to 12. Until then, baby teeth do these jobs.

  • Chewing: They help your child chew all kinds of foods.
  • Speech: While your child is learning to talk, they help make sounds clear.
  • Holding space: They hold space for the permanent teeth growing underneath.

If a cavity is left alone, these things can happen.

  • Pain and infection: As a cavity gets deeper, it hurts, and the gum can swell or form pus.
  • Eating and sleeping: Many kids with severe cavities have pain and trouble eating and sleeping. If it hurts to eat, nutrition can suffer, and it can get in the way of preschool and learning. Improvements in sleep, eating, and pain have been reported after treatment.
  • Permanent teeth: Infection at the tip of a baby tooth's root can cause spots or eruption problems (problems with the tooth coming in) in the permanent tooth growing underneath.
  • The bite: Losing baby teeth early can leave less room for the permanent teeth, so they may come in crooked.
  • New cavities: Kids with cavities are at higher risk of cavities in other baby teeth and in permanent teeth.

Just because your child doesn't say it hurts doesn't mean everything's fine. Young kids aren't good at putting discomfort into words, so it may show up as chewing on only one side or avoiding hard foods.

Today, cavities are "managed"

Cavities with a hole used to be drilled and filled as a rule. Now the approach is "caries management": choosing among several options based on how deep the cavity is and how well your child can cooperate.

  • Applying fluoride to keep an early cavity from getting worse.
  • Painting on medicine that stops the cavity from progressing.
  • Placing a temporary filling to buy time.
  • Filling the tooth, or doing nerve treatment if it's deep.

So not every cavity needs drilling right away. But whether to watch or treat isn't something to decide at home. The dentist decides after checking the tooth. Here's what parents can do.

  1. Have even cavities in baby teeth checked by a dentist. Don't put it off because it's "going to fall out anyway."
  2. Ask about options and their pros and cons. Find out which approach suits your child's age and ability to cooperate, and what to expect from each.
  3. If a baby tooth was pulled early, ask whether a space maintainer is needed. It's a device that holds the space for the permanent tooth.
  4. After treatment, change daily habits too. Keep managing how often your child has sweet snacks, and brushing. If habits stay the same, cavities come back.

If the gums swell, you see pus, your child wakes at night in pain, or chews on only one side, see a dentist without waiting for the next checkup.

Frequently asked questions

My child is so young. I don't think they can handle treatment.

That's understandable. Whether and how to treat depends on your child's age, how deep the cavity is, and how well they can cooperate. Gentler options, like medicine that stops progression or a temporary filling, can sometimes buy time, so talk it over with your dentist.

Do cavities in the front teeth need treatment too?

Front teeth also help with chewing and speech, and they hold space until the permanent teeth come in. Rather than leaving them because they're front teeth, have them checked and decide on an approach with your dentist.

If my child doesn't say it hurts, isn't it fine?

Cavities can get worse without any pain. Even if your child doesn't say anything, regular checkups to keep an eye on things are important.

We had a cavity treated, and now there's another one.

Kids who have had cavities are at higher risk of new ones. Review how often your child has sweet snacks, brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, and bedtime brushing, and talk with your dentist about prevention.

Get care right away if

  • The gums swell or you see pus
  • Your child wakes at night with tooth pain
  • Your child chews only on one side or avoids hard foods
  • The face swells or there's also a fever
  • You see a hole in a tooth, or a brown or black area that's getting bigger
