Fluoride Varnish for Kids: How It Works and How Often to Get It

Fluoride varnish is concentrated fluoride painted on at the dentist. How it cuts cavities in baby teeth, how often, aftercare, and what to ask about cavities.

At a glance

  • Brushed on in moments, with no drilling or numbing
  • Pooled studies show about 37% fewer cavities in baby teeth
  • It doesn't replace fluoride toothpaste at home

At a checkup, the dentist may ask, "Would you like fluoride varnish today?" You might wonder why it's needed if you already brush with fluoride toothpaste at home, and whether it's okay for your child. Here's what fluoride varnish is, why it's used, and how often your child might need it.

What fluoride varnish is and how it helps

Fluoride varnish is a concentrated fluoride painted onto the tooth surface. A sticky liquid is brushed thinly onto the teeth, where it stays for a while as the fluoride slowly soaks in. Fluoride makes the outer layer of the tooth harder and helps spots that have started to dissolve from acid harden again.

It only takes a moment at the dentist. There's no drilling or numbing, and it doesn't hurt. It's a kind of prevention that even young children who can't lie in the dental chair for long can have.

There's a reason it's especially useful for young kids. Baby teeth have a thinner outer layer (enamel) than adult teeth, so cavities move fast once they start. And at this age, no matter how hard you try, it's tough to reach every nook when brushing. Varnish helps fill in some of those gaps. Pooled studies show varnish reduced cavities in baby teeth by about 37%.

There's one key thing to remember. Varnish doesn't replace fluoride toothpaste at home. Brushing with fluoride toothpaste every day is the foundation, and varnish is an extra layer of prevention added regularly at the dental office. Getting varnish doesn't mean you can take a break from brushing or give more sweet snacks.

Here are a few common myths.

  • "No cavities means no need for varnish": Even without cavities, a child at high risk may get it more often. Varnish is prevention used before cavities form.
  • "One application lasts a long time": To keep the benefit going, it needs to be reapplied regularly.
  • "The more fluoride, the better": At the dentist, varnish is used only in the amount the dentist decides. There's no need to use more toothpaste at home.

Talk it over at your checkup

  1. Ask how often. It's usually every 3 to 6 months, and the dentist decides based on your child's cavity risk.
  2. If your child's risk is high, stay on top of it. Children at high risk are advised to have it at least every 6 months. If your child has had cavities before, often has sweet snacks, or still has night feeds, mention it at the checkup.
  3. Follow the instructions afterward. Instructions about eating, drinking, and brushing afterward differ a little from office to office. Follow what you're told.
  4. Keep up your care at home. After varnish, keep brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste and managing sweet snacks.
  5. If you're told there's a cavity, ask about options. Ask about treatment options and the pros and cons of each, and decide together.

If it's your child's first time and it feels strange, keep it short and easy, like "The dentist is going to put a shiny coat on your teeth." Practicing keeping the mouth open at home ahead of time helps too.

Frequently asked questions

We use fluoride toothpaste at home. Do we need varnish too?

They do different jobs. Toothpaste is your everyday fluoride, and varnish is a concentrated fluoride added regularly at the dentist. Whether your child needs it, and how often, is decided by the dentist based on cavity risk.

Is it okay if my child swallows some varnish?

Varnish is recommended prevention even for young children, and since it's painted on thinly, the amount is small. Just follow the dental office's instructions for afterward.

If there's already a cavity, is varnish enough?

It depends on how deep the cavity is and its condition. An early cavity, before a hole forms, can be stopped from getting worse with fluoride, but a cavity that has gone further may need other treatment. Ask the dentist what treatment options there are and the pros and cons of each, and decide together.

Until what age is varnish used?

Rather than a set end age, cavity risk is the guide. If the risk is high, it may be done more often. If it's low, the interval may be longer. It's decided again at each checkup.

When to see a dentist

  • Your child's teeth are in, but you haven't talked with a dentist about fluoride varnish yet
  • You see a white band or a chalky, dull patch on a tooth
  • A brown or black spot appears on a tooth
  • Your child has had cavities before, or siblings have lots of cavities
  • It's been a while since the last varnish and you need to set the next one
