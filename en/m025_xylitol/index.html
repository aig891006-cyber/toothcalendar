Does Xylitol Help Prevent Cavities in Kids? What the Evidence Says Now

Xylitol is less harmful than sugar, but the evidence for it as a cavity preventive is weak. What we know now, tips on candy and gum, and keeping it from dogs.

At a glance

  • Think of xylitol as a less harmful sweet, not a preventive
  • Fluoride toothpaste and fewer sweets come first
  • Xylitol can be dangerous for dogs, so keep it out of reach

Many parents think of xylitol gum or candy as a "tooth-friendly snack." When your child wants something sweet, you might wonder if a xylitol product is a good choice. Here's what people used to think, what the evidence shows now, and how to think about it at home.

Xylitol: then and now

Xylitol is a sweetener used in place of sugar. Mouth bacteria can't easily turn it into acid the way they do with sugar, so it doesn't cause cavities. That's why, for a long time, it was known as a snack that prevents cavities.

Today, the thinking has shifted a bit. "Doesn't cause cavities" and "prevents cavities" aren't the same thing. When studies are pooled, the evidence that xylitol prevents cavities is weak and inconsistent.

  • The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) retired its xylitol policy in 2025. It didn't give a reason.
  • Even before that, its policy said the amounts used in studies, 5–10 g a day split over 3–5 times, weren't practical.
  • A benefit was seen when children at high risk of cavities chewed 5–6 g or more a day, more than 3 times a day, as gum.
  • Xylitol candies and gummies didn't show a benefit, and most of the evidence is of moderate or low quality.

Retiring the policy doesn't mean xylitol is harmful, though. It's best thought of as "a less harmful sweet than sugar," not as "a cavity preventive."

Let's clear up a couple of common myths. There was an idea that if mom chews xylitol gum, fewer cavity germs pass to the child, but that hasn't been settled either. And just because a product contains xylitol doesn't mean it's a snack your child can have as much as they like. Too much can cause stomach pain or diarrhea.

How to think about it at home

  1. Fluoride toothpaste is the basis of cavity prevention. Brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste and having sweet snacks less often come first. Xylitol can't replace either one.
  2. There's no need to give xylitol candies or gummies on purpose. Since there's no proven benefit, there's no reason to seek them out "because they're good for teeth."
  3. If your child is having a sweet anyway, think of it as a less harmful option. It's fine to choose it instead of a sugary candy.
  4. For gum, consider your child's age and chewing skills first. Gum can be swallowed or cause choking. Don't give gum or hard candy to children under 4. Save xylitol gum for when your child is old enough to chew it and spit it out without swallowing it.
  5. Don't let your child have too much. Too much can cause stomach pain or diarrhea.

Xylitol can be life-threatening to dogs. Keep xylitol gum, candy, and toothpaste out of your dog's reach, and call a vet right away if your dog eats any.

If you have a dog, take a second look at bags, the table, and gum containers in your child's hands. A dog could also eat a candy your child dropped.

Frequently asked questions

At what age can a child chew xylitol gum?

Don't give gum to children under 4. Even after that, what matters is whether your child can chew gum and spit it out without swallowing it. Gum can cause choking, so if you're not sure, it's safer not to give it yet. If you're worried, talk with your dentist or pediatrician.

If my child eats xylitol candy, will they not get cavities?

Xylitol candies and gummies haven't been shown to prevent cavities. They're just less harmful than sugar candy, so keep up brushing with fluoride toothpaste and limiting how often your child snacks.

What about xylitol toothpaste?

The most important thing in a toothpaste is whether it contains fluoride. Check the fluoride level and use the right amount, rather than looking for xylitol. That's a rice-grain smear under age 3, and a pea-sized amount from age 3.

If the policy was retired, does that mean xylitol is harmful?

No. The reason for retiring it wasn't given, and it shouldn't be taken to mean xylitol is harmful. But too much can upset the stomach, so watch the amount.

When to see a dentist

  • You're wondering whether xylitol has a place in your child's cavity prevention
  • Your child gets cavities often and you want a prevention plan that fits
  • Your child says a tooth is sensitive, or you see a brown stain or white band
  • Stomach pain or diarrhea continues after eating xylitol products (check with your pediatrician too)
