Getting Through Candy Season Without Cavity Worries: How Much and How Kids Eat Candy Both Matter

With candy, how much and how kids eat it both matter, especially not spreading it out. Eating it in one sitting, candies to skip, and the leftover stash.

At a glance

  • With candy, how much and how it's eaten both matter
  • Eat it once, at a set time, not all day
  • Brush before bed, then nothing but water

There are times when candy comes pouring in all at once, like Halloween, or days when kids trade stick-shaped snacks. Kids are thrilled, but parents worry about cavities. Since it's hard to keep candy away completely, knowing how to eat it in a way that's easier on teeth can bring a lot of peace of mind.

How much and how long kids eat both matter

Bacteria in the mouth feed on sugar and make acid. That acid slowly dissolves the tooth surface, and that's how cavities begin. Luckily, saliva washes the acid away and hardens the tooth surface again, but that takes time.

So for teeth, both the amount of candy and how often it's eaten matter, and it's especially important not to spread it out, so that sugar doesn't stay in the mouth for long. Nibbling candy all day keeps the teeth bathed in acid, with no break for saliva to help them recover. Eating one piece, then another a little later, means new acid forms each time. Even with the same amount, eating it once at a set time and being done is far better. That doesn't mean it's fine to eat a lot just because it's in one sitting. The idea that "spreading it out in small bits is less harmful" is actually backwards. A short reminder for kids, like "Candy all at once, at candy time," is easy for them to remember.

The type of candy makes a difference too:

  • Sticky candy: Chewy things like caramels and gummies stay stuck between teeth and in the grooves of the molars for a long time.
  • Candy that stays in the mouth: Lollipops and hard candy keep releasing sugar into the mouth until they melt.
  • Candy that melts fast: Chocolate melts quickly in the mouth and washes off. If you have to pick, it's a better choice than sticky candy.

One sitting, then done

Instead of banning candy, set rules for eating it. Clear rules mean fewer battles with your child, too.

  1. Have candy just once, right after a meal or at one set snack time. Mealtime saliva helps wash the acid away.
  2. Steer away from sticky candy like caramels and gummies, and candy that stays in the mouth a long time, like lollipops. Don't give hard or sticky candy or gum to children under 4, since it can be a choking hazard.
  3. Afterward, drink water or rinse the mouth with water.
  4. Keep the leftover candy out of sight, and hand it out only at snack time, a little at a time.
  5. Brush with fluoride toothpaste before bed. Use a rice-grain smear under age 3 and a pea-sized amount from age 3.
  6. After bedtime brushing, nothing but water.

The first night of the season is especially tricky. Kids get excited and eat candy late into the evening, which makes it easy to skip brushing. Let your child have that day's share after dinner, then put the candy away and stick to the usual brushing time.

It's also nice to sort through the candy together with your child. Let them pick a few favorites and share or set aside the rest. That way, kids feel they made the choice themselves.

A common mistake parents make is leaving the candy bag in a child's room or on the dining table. When it's in sight, little hands reach for it all day. And a consistent rule of a little at the same time every day works better for kids and their teeth than an on-and-off rule like "You had a lot today, so none tomorrow."

Never let your child fall asleep with candy in their mouth or eat candy after bedtime brushing.

United States guidance

US: Halloween

Serve Halloween candy at a set time right after a meal rather than throughout the day, and skip sticky or long-lasting candies such as caramels and lollipops. Keep leftover candy out of sight or use a local candy buy-back event, and never skip brushing before bed.

Frequently asked questions

How many pieces of candy a day is okay?

There's no set number, but both how much and how often matter. Even with the same amount, eating it all at once at a set time and being done is easier on teeth. That doesn't mean it's fine to eat a lot in one sitting.

Does my child need to brush right after eating candy?

After eating candy, have them drink water or rinse with water. The most important brushing of the day is the one before bed, with fluoride toothpaste.

Can my child eat as much chocolate as they want?

Chocolate melts quickly and washes off, so it's a better choice than sticky candy. But it still has sugar, so like other candy, have it all at once at a set time and rinse with water afterward.

We have way too much leftover candy.

Keep it out of sight and bring out just a little at snack time. If your area has an event that collects leftover candy, that's another option.

When to see the dentist

  • Your child says a tooth feels sensitive or hurts when eating candy
  • You see brown or black spots on the teeth
  • A chalky white, dull area shows up on a tooth
  • A filling or crown came off while eating sticky candy
  • Candy season is over, and it's been a long time since the last checkup
