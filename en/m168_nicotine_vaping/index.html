Teens, Smoking, Vaping, and Nicotine Pouches: Effects on the Mouth and How to Talk

Vapes and nicotine pouches are tobacco too. How nicotine affects a growing brain and the gums, how to open a talk without lecturing, and signs to see a dentist.

At a glance

  • Vapes and nicotine pouches are tobacco products too
  • Start by asking what they think, not by lecturing
  • A white or red patch that doesn't heal needs a dentist

Finding an unfamiliar device in your child's bag, or hearing that vapes are going around their friend group, can make your heart sink. You may wonder whether to come down hard or pretend you didn't notice. Here's how vapes and nicotine pouches affect the mouth and body, and how to start the conversation with your teen.

The biggest trap is thinking it's less harmful

The teen years are when many kids first try tobacco. Vapes and nicotine pouches (small pouches tucked between the gum and lip) in particular don't make smoke and come in fruit or mint flavors, so they're often seen as less harmful. But these products are tobacco too, and most contain nicotine. No tobacco product is safe for children and teens.

Nicotine is highly addictive. Even with a sweet flavor, the nicotine is just as addictive. Nicotine can also harm the still-developing brain, especially the parts in charge of attention, learning, mood, and impulse control. Some people also use vape devices to inhale substances other than nicotine.

In the mouth, these changes can happen:

  • Gum disease: The gums swell, bleed, or stay inflamed for a long time.
  • Dry mouth: With less saliva, the mouth can't rinse itself as well, which also makes cavities and bad breath more likely.
  • Staining: Yellowish stains appear on the teeth and tongue.
  • Gum recession: Placing a nicotine pouch in the same spot every time can irritate the gums there and make them recede.

Tobacco is also a major cause of oral cancer. It won't happen right away, but remember that the earlier someone starts, the longer they're exposed.

Ask first, lecture less

Teens tend to shut down when they get a lecture. The goal of the conversation isn't to "win." It's to give your teen the information to make their own decision.

  1. Pick a relaxed moment when you don't have to sit face to face, like in the car or on a walk.
  2. Ask what they think first, like "What do your friends think about vaping these days?" Listen all the way through, and don't argue right away.
  3. Share the facts short and clear. "Even flavored ones have nicotine, and it's addictive. And it leaves its mark on your gums and teeth, too."
  4. Don't try to cover it all in one talk. Short follow-ups whenever it comes up in the news or an ad stick better.
  5. If you use tobacco yourself, offering to quit together is the strongest lesson.

It also helps to make sure cigarette smoke and vape devices aren't easy to come across at home. How the adults around your teen act has more influence than you might think.

A common mistake is opening with the evidence and backing your teen into a corner. Then they learn to hide it first. If you find out they've already started, set your disappointment aside for a moment and ask, "Do you want to quit?" Many teens who smoke or vape actually want to stop.

If your teen has already started, help works better than punishment. Pediatricians and dentists can offer quitting support.

Frequently asked questions

They say their vape has no nicotine. Is that okay?

Nicotine has been found in some products labeled nicotine-free. And even without nicotine, a vape isn't safe for teens. Treat every product as unsafe for teens, since no tobacco product is safe for them, and steer the conversation toward not using any.

Nicotine pouches don't make smoke. Aren't they only a little harmful to the mouth?

Nicotine is absorbed into the body through the lining of the mouth, so they're just as addictive. They can also irritate the gums where the pouch sits and cause the gums to recede.

Is it okay to bring up tobacco at the dentist?

Yes, dentists can help with quitting too. If your teen finds it hard to talk in front of you, giving them some time alone with the dentist during the visit can help.

What if my teen says, "But you smoke too"?

Admit it honestly and say, "That's why I know how hard it is to quit." Trying to quit together is more convincing than anything you could say.

When to see a dentist

  • A white or red patch in the mouth doesn't heal
  • The gums swell often or bleed when brushing
  • The gums where a pouch was placed have receded, making the teeth look longer
  • The mouth is often dry, or yellowish stains on the teeth are increasing
  • Your teen wants to quit and needs help
