Is Sedation or General Anesthesia Safe for Kids' Dental Work? Research and Key Questions

Many studies found one short anesthetic made no difference in IQ. What the research says, safety questions about sedation, and how to prepare for the day.

At a glance

  • Studies found one short anesthetic made no difference in IQ
  • Ask who monitors your child and what emergency gear is on hand
  • Don't accept sedative medicine to give at home

When a child has many cavities or is too young to get through treatment, the dentist may suggest sedation or general anesthesia. Lots of parents then worry, "Could anesthesia at such a young age harm my child's brain?" Here's what the research shows so far, and what to check if it's recommended.

Anesthesia and brain development: what we know

The worry started with a 2016 warning from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). It said repeated or lengthy (more than 3 hours) use of anesthetic drugs in children under 3 may affect brain development. The warning came mainly from animal studies, and what it meant for people wasn't clear. In the same announcement, the FDA also said a single, short exposure is unlikely to harm behavior or learning.

Since then, large studies in people have followed.

  • A study comparing infants who had a general anesthetic of just under 1 hour with infants who had an awake regional anesthetic found no difference in IQ as they grew up.
  • A study comparing children who had anesthesia when young with siblings who didn't also found no difference in IQ.
  • A study of children who had one or more anesthetics also found no difference in IQ. Kids with multiple anesthetics did show small differences in some fine motor skills, reading speed, and parent-reported behavior problems, but whether that was from anesthesia or something else isn't clear.

In short, today's concern centers on repeated or long anesthesia at a young age, and many studies found that one short anesthetic made no difference in IQ. But these results don't guarantee that all sedation or anesthesia is safe. That's why it's important to check the safety measures below. No particular drug has been shown to be safer than others.

One more thing to know: sedation runs on a continuum. Even when light sedation is intended, some children may go deeper. That's why who is watching your child and what equipment is used to monitor them matter more for safety than which drug is used.

If it's suggested, check and prepare like this

It helps to bring the questions below to your consultation. Asking is completely normal.

  • How deep will the sedation be? Who gives the medicine, and who watches my child?
  • For deep sedation or general anesthesia, is there a separate anesthesia professional who only watches my child, apart from whoever is doing the treatment?
  • Will oxygen levels, heart rate, and breathing be monitored continuously? Are emergency equipment and staff trained in emergency care on hand?
  • Will everything be treated this time, or will more anesthesia be needed? How long will it take?
  • Could fluoride application, medicine that stops cavities, or temporary fillings delay sedation? What are the risks of waiting?

On the day of treatment, do this.

  1. Follow the clinic's fasting instructions exactly. AAPD guidance is to stop clear liquids 2 hours before, breast milk 4 hours before, and formula, milk, or a light meal 6 hours before. An empty stomach lowers the risk of vomit going into the airway during anesthesia.
  2. Don't leave the treatment area while your child is sedated.
  3. If you can, have 2 adults travel home together, so one can drive and the other can watch only your child's breathing.
  4. Have an adult stay with your child until they've fully recovered. If your child falls asleep at home, lay them on their side and check their breathing often. If their breathing seems wrong or you can't easily wake them, call your local emergency number right away.

Don't accept sedative medicine to give at home before the visit. Giving sedatives without medical monitoring is dangerous, and deaths have been reported on the way to the clinic.

Frequently asked questions

Can't we put off treatment to avoid anesthesia?

It's good to ask whether there are ways to delay. But putting it off too long raises the risk of pain and infection. Decide together with the care team based on your child's condition.

My child has a bit of a cold. Should we go ahead as planned?

A cough or runny nose can put extra strain on the airway. Don't just go ahead. Let the clinic know before treatment and talk about whether to postpone.

What if we're told several anesthetics are needed?

Ask whether treatment can be finished in one session, and why it would need to be split up. The small differences seen with multiple anesthetics have no clear cause, so talk through the need and the number of sessions with the care team.

What's the difference between sedation and general anesthesia?

Sedation uses medicine to make your child relaxed and sleepy, while general anesthesia means treatment happens while your child is fully asleep. Either way, the deeper it goes, the more a separate monitoring person and equipment matter.

Talk to the care team right away if

  • You were given sedative medicine to give at home before treatment (don't give it; ask first)
  • Your child has a fever, cough, or runny nose on the day of treatment
  • You weren't able to follow the fasting times
  • After going home, your child's breathing sounds unusual or they don't wake up as usual (call your local emergency number right away)
  • While waiting for treatment, your child has tooth pain or swollen gums
